## CarGurus Data Breach Exposes 12.1 Million Accounts
CarGurus, a popular online car marketplace, confirmed a data breach that exposed information tied to about **12.1 million user accounts**.

The exposed data may include **full names, physical addresses, and loan pre-qualification information and outcomes** from the platform. While the breach did not reportedly include banking or payment card data, the information is still valuable to criminals.

Attackers can use details like this to craft highly believable phishing messages; for example, pretending to follow up about a car loan or financing offer you previously looked into.

Because the data relates to vehicle shopping and financing, scammers could target victims with messages about **loan approvals, dealership offers, or account issues**.

___

### What to Know
- Around 12.1 million CarGurus accounts were impacted.
- Exposed information includes names, addresses, and loan pre-qualification data.
- Even without credit card numbers, this type of data is useful for targeted phishing scams.
- Attackers may impersonate lenders, dealerships, or CarGurus itself.

### What to Do Now
- Be cautious of emails, texts, or calls about car loans, financing approvals, or dealership offers.
- Avoid clicking links in unexpected messages claiming to be from CarGurus or lenders.
- Monitor your credit reports and financial accounts for unusual activity.
- If you've used CarGurus, watch for official breach notifications with further instructions.

## Phishing Campaign Targets LastPass Users  
Separately, researchers are warning about a phishing campaign targeting users of the password manager LastPass.

Victims receive emails claiming their master password was changed or that there was unauthorized activity on their account. The message appears to come from LastPass because it uses the LastPass display name and the body of the email looks convincing, but the actual sender email address is unrelated to the company.

The goal is to **trick users into revealing their master password**, which would give attackers access to the entire password vault.

### What to Know
- Attackers are impersonating LastPass in phishing emails.
- The email may claim your password was changed or that your account is compromised.
- The sender address is not actually from LastPass, even though the name looks legitimate.
- The scam attempts to steal your master password.

### What to Do Now
- Never share your master password with anyone. Legitimate services will never ask for it.
- Always check the actual sender email address, not just the display name.
- If you receive a suspicious message, report it to abuse@lastpass.com.
- Access your account only by visiting the official LastPass website directly.
